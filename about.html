About

I’m Yifan Bian.

I’m a PhD researcher in Experimental Psychology at University College London, supervised by Professor Eva Krumhuber. I study how faces show emotion and how people perceive them. I also study how AI can read a face together with its context, from face-analysis tools to multimodal large language models (AI that understands images and language together).

What I work on

My research is about a gap. Much emotion research, and much of what emotion AI learns from, still relies on people acting out emotions for a camera. Real reactions are subtler, more varied and harder to label. So I find and describe recordings of genuine expressions, and test how well emotion AI copes with them. I also work out what it would take for AI to read a face in context, and to say what it can’t tell.

I also build tools. One, a searchable table of 128 facial expression databases, was published with our review. Four more are in development; I design and build them myself, with Claude Code as my pair programmer.

Facial expression science

How faces show emotion in real life

Spontaneous versus posed expressions, the Facial Action Coding System (FACS), the standard way to describe facial movements, and what different labels really measure: what a person felt, what an observer saw, what a machine scored.

Shown in: my four papers · Face Value

Systematic reviews and data curation at scale
  • 13,900 papers screened down to 128 databases;
  • 32 details per database, in a scheme designed with co-authors;
  • availability checked with database authors.

Shown in: Emotion Review, 2026 · Facial Expression Database Finder

Evaluating AI

Testing models on the data that matters

A cross-database test of a commercial emotion AI on genuine expressions. Accuracy was broken down by emotion, database and gender, and the model’s decisions were traced to the facial movements it relied on. I designed the method with a co-author and ran all the analyses.

Shown in: Electronics, 2026

Statistics
  • regression, ANOVA and significance tests with corrections for multiple comparisons (Electronics, 2026);
  • grouped cross-validation, permutation tests and mixed-effects models, with matching R and Python export (Face Value’s built-in statistics lab);
  • within-person mixed-effects models, lagged correlations and randomised experiments on oneself (Self-Discovery).

Building with Claude

I design and build my tools with Claude Code as a pair programmer. I set the purpose of each tool, the rules for what counts as evidence and the checks a result must pass. Claude and I write the code together, and I write down what each tool can’t do.

It means I can turn a research idea into a working tool quickly, while keeping the standard of evidence I’d expect from a paper. Crossread is an example. Its first version went into code on 16 September 2026, and by late October it was running about twenty analyses from more than a dozen published models, with automated tests.
