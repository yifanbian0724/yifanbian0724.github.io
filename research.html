Research

Real faces, read in context

Much emotion research, and much of what emotion AI learns from, still uses posed faces: people acting out an emotion for a camera. Real reactions are subtler and more varied. My research asks what genuine faces actually do, and how well people and machines can read them.

FindFirst authorEmotion Review, 2026

128 databases of genuine facial expressions, and the gaps they share

Bian, Y., Kim, H., & Krumhuber, E. G. (2026). A systematic review of 128 spontaneous and dynamic facial expression databases. Emotion Review. https://doi.org/10.1177/17540739261478876

Read the paper Search the 128 databases

Open access · CC BY-NC 4.0

People who study real expressions, and engineers who train emotion AI, need recordings of genuine reactions. Those recordings are scattered across thousands of papers.

We ran a systematic review: a search of the research that follows fixed, documented rules. We screened the titles and abstracts of 13,900 papers, read 741 in full, and found 128 publicly available video databases of spontaneous facial expressions. We recorded 32 details per database, such as its emotions, its camera set-up and how to get access, and published them as a searchable table.

What we found

  • Together the databases cover 84 kinds of emotions and other states (such as pain, boredom or confusion), and more than 11,800 people, from 3 to 2,222 per database.
  • Self-conscious feelings such as pride and shame appear in only 4% of them.
  • 105 of the 128 were recorded in a lab, most with the camera facing the person.
  • To bring emotions about, researchers used everything from Super Bowl commercials and comedy shows to a cold-water task, an unpleasant smell and a mock crime interview.

What we recommend

Future databases should:

  • cover a wider range of emotions;
  • say clearly whether a label reflects what a person felt or what an observer saw;
  • film people interacting with each other;
  • keep trained human coders, who label facial movements by hand, as a check on automated tools, for both accuracy and bias;
  • recruit more diverse participants;
  • report how clips were chosen.

Data should also fit the question. For the timing of facial movements, 30 people filmed at 500 frames per second may tell you more than 2,000 filmed at 25.

TestFirst authorElectronics, 2026

Emotion AI reads the textbook, not the face

Bian, Y., Kim, H., & Krumhuber, E. G. (2026). A cross-corpus evaluation on spontaneous and dynamic facial expressions for automated emotion classification. Electronics, 15(4), 849. https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics15040849

Read the paper

Open access · CC BY 4.0

Emotion AI is often tested on posed faces, or on one database at a time, where it can look very accurate. Posed faces tend to follow the “textbook” face for each emotion: the standard, exaggerated version people produce when asked to act it out.

We tested a commercial system, AFFDEX 1.0, on 250 genuine expressions of five emotions, from five databases of spontaneous expressions, with equal numbers of men and women. AFFDEX itself was trained on one of the largest collections of spontaneous expressions.

What we found

  • Overall it was right 34.4% of the time. Guessing at random among the five emotions would score 20%.
  • It recognised happiness 94% of the time, disgust and surprise 24%, fear 16% and sadness 14%.
  • It labelled 61.7% of fear clips and 58.7% of disgust clips as happiness. When it said “happy”, it was right only 35.6% of the time.
  • It was more accurate on men’s faces (40.0%) than on women’s (28.8%). For disgust, the gap was 40% against 8%.
  • Its decisions leaned on one or two textbook movements per emotion. About three quarters (74%) of what drove its happiness score came from a single movement, the lip-corner pull of a smile. Yet over 90% of the genuine expressions were dominated by other, non-textbook movements.

My role. I designed the method with Hyunwoo Kim and ran all the analyses.

What it means. Test emotion AI on genuine expressions from several databases, report how accurate it is for different groups of people, and treat emotions as blends rather than single labels. The same kind of test can be run on any emotion-recognition system.

FindCo-authorAffective Science, 2025

Psychology still studies emotion mostly with posed faces

Kim, H., Bian, Y., & Krumhuber, E. G. (2025). A review of 25 spontaneous and dynamic facial expression databases of basic emotions. Affective Science, 6(2), 380–394. https://doi.org/10.1007/s42761-024-00289-3

Read the paper PubMed Central

Open access · CC BY 4.0

In a review led by Hyunwoo Kim, which I co-authored, we described 25 databases of genuine, moving expressions of basic emotions (such as happiness, fear and anger) that researchers can obtain. We compared what they capture, who was filmed and how to get access.

We also looked at 13 journals from 2020 to 2024. In 12 of them, most articles on basic emotions used posed faces. In the journal Emotion, 84% used posed faces and 12% spontaneous ones. Only Transactions on Affective Computing used more spontaneous faces (54%) than posed ones (46%).

ExplainFirst authorSensors, 2024 (online December 2023)

Real-world faces need context, and new AI can help

Bian, Y., Küster, D., Liu, H., & Krumhuber, E. G. (2024). Understanding naturalistic facial expressions with deep learning and multimodal large language models. Sensors, 24(1), 126. https://doi.org/10.3390/s24010126

Read the paper PubMed Central

Published online December 2023 · Open access · CC BY 4.0

Faces outside the lab are messy. Heads turn and the light changes. Context changes what a face means, too: in a wedding scene, a single object such as a gun can turn a reading of joy into one of fear.

With colleagues at the University of Bremen, we compared five recent, easy-to-use face-analysis toolkits:

  • OpenFace 2.0
  • Py-Feat
  • Affdex 2.0
  • LibreFace
  • PyAFAR

We argued that multimodal large language models (AI that understands images and language together) could do three things:

  • read a face together with its context;
  • explain their reasoning;
  • learn new emotion categories from a few examples.

We were just as clear about the limits:

  • it is not yet clear whether they can match the precision of trained human coders who label facial movements by hand;
  • it is not yet clear how closely they agree with human perception;
  • context can add noise as well as meaning;
  • one of the paper’s own examples showed a model describing something that wasn’t in the picture.
