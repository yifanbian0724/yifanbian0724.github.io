Facial Expression Database Finder

Search 128 video databases of spontaneous (unposed) facial expressions.

Download the table (OSF) Read the paper

OSF can’t preview the table, so it downloads as a file. Open it in a web browser on a computer, with an internet connection.

About the table

Published August 2026 · version 1

Searching for “pain”, switching to the Technical tab, sorting by number of people and, on wider screens, opening a database’s card. Screen recording, about 14 seconds, no sound.

Recorded from the published table (OSF version 1).

Paused because your device asks for less motion.

Researchers and engineers who need recordings of genuine facial expressions, whether to run an experiment or to train and test emotion AI. These databases are scattered across thousands of papers. The Finder puts the 128 publicly available databases our review found into one searchable table, with 32 details per database:

  • what emotions it holds and how they were brought about;
  • who was filmed, and how;
  • how to get access.

  • It comes from a systematic review. We screened 13,900 papers, read 741 in full and included 128 databases. Three of us described every database and checked each other’s work.
  • It’s practical. Alongside the science, each entry says how to get the data: where it lives, how access works, and whether its authors confirmed it was still available when we last checked (May 2025).
  • It’s precise. Most columns come with a written definition, so you know exactly what a term means in this table.
  • It’s published and citable. It is Supplementary Table S1 of our review in Emotion Review (2026).

  1. Type a word such as pain or EEG and the table narrows as you type. Each tab searches its own columns, and a filter set on one tab carries to the others.
  2. Pick an emotion label from the list to see the databases that use it.
  3. Sort by number of people to bring the largest collections to the top.
  4. Click a database’s name for a card with everything about it, from how emotions were brought about to the camera set-up.

Status

Published in August 2026 with our review in Emotion Review, and archived on OSF (the Open Science Framework, a free archive for research files) as Supplementary Table S1. Availability was last checked with database authors in May 2025.

Crossread

Read a recording’s faces, voices and words side by side, and see where they disagree.

Ask me for a demo

I’ll show it to you over a video call.

Updated November 2026

Crossread on President Bill Clinton’s remarks at the White House on 26 January 1998, in one continuous session, as he says “I did not have sexual relations with that woman”.

  • The main screen labels each facial movement on his face and reads it against his own usual range, beside his eyes, his voice and the transcript.
  • A click on a movement, eyelids tightened, opens its card beside the stage: the frame where it was strongest with the eye region boxed, then the whole face, and the movement’s trajectory over the whole speech with its five strongest moments; a click on one goes back to the denial.
  • Compare puts the denial beside the 26 seconds before it, every channel side by side. Then the map of moments, all channels at once, and the whole recording as a clock with a stretch magnified.

Still, because your device asks for less motion.

Researchers who study recorded conversations and interviews, in psychology, human–computer interaction and beyond. Also teams that need to know what a face, a voice and the words were doing at each moment before deciding what it means.

You add a video of people talking. Crossread runs more than a dozen published AI models on your Mac and lines up what they find on one timeline:

  • which facial muscles moved;
  • how the head and body moved;
  • how loud and how high each voice was;
  • what was said;
  • what several emotion models make of the face, the voice and the words.

  • It never blends everything into a single “emotion score”. Face, voice and words each keep their own model and their own timeline, so you can see where they agree and where they don’t. When a test face says “I did not expect this at all” in a computer voice, a text model reads the words as surprise, a voice model hears only neutral, and Crossread shows both.
  • It compares each person with themselves. Readings are shown against that person’s own usual range, not a crowd average.
  • It says what it can’t tell you. Every model comes with a model card that lists its limits. The app also marks which analyses a recording can support, and says why when it can’t: for example, when an analysis needs at least 20 seconds and the recording has 19.
  • It’s private. Everything runs on your Mac, and nothing is uploaded.

  1. See which muscles moved. The face is drawn as a turning 3D map of glowing dots that lights up where each movement happened, named in plain words.
  2. Read a conversation line by line. Each sentence becomes a card showing what the words say, how the voice sounded and what the face did, side by side.
  3. Find out who said what. It matches voices to faces by checking whether a mouth moved while a voice spoke, and flags lines it isn’t sure about.
  4. Ask plain questions, for example how long a person spoke and how loud and high their voice was, and see which questions this recording can actually answer.

A test with a known answer

A computer-generated test face, animated with movements scripted in advance, was run through Crossread, so the right answer was known. Each facial movement has a standard number in the Facial Action Coding System (FACS), the standard way to describe facial movements. That is what the “AU” codes in these pictures are: 12, for example, is the lip-corner pull of a smile.

Synthetic face: ICT Face Model Light from ICT-FaceKit (USC Institute for Creative Technologies), MIT licence.

Status

In development for Mac and not released. It analyses a recording after you add it, not live, and it handles speech in English and Mandarin. The test face is computer-generated; the real-world example uses footage courtesy of NASA/JPL-Caltech.

How it’s builtDesigned and built by me with Claude Code since September 2026 · Python back end (FastAPI) running about twenty analyses (October 2026) from more than a dozen published models on Apple silicon (PyTorch, ONNX Runtime, MLX) · matches voices to faces by lip movement · runs a vision-language model on the laptop · web interface with WebGL · automated tests

Literature Galaxy

See a research library as a galaxy of papers, and where they agree and disagree.

Try the live preview

Preview snapshot, October 2026 · opens the tool in development

Updated November 2026

Each point is a document in my research library, placed near documents whose full text covers similar ground and coloured by topic group. Drag to turn it.

Drawn in your browser from the library as of October 2026.

Still, because your device asks for less motion.

Topic groups in the map, largest first

    Students and researchers finding their way around facial expression and emotion research, and anyone curious about how science argues.

    Papers from my research library are placed next to papers whose full text covers similar ground, so topics gather into clusters you can see. AI (Claude) read each paper’s full text, where it could be read, for what it argues. Where a paper takes a side (about half do), the AI linked it to statements the field keeps returning to, such as whether smiles that crinkle the eyes reveal real enjoyment. Each link records whether the paper supports the statement, partly agrees with it or challenges it.

    • It maps disagreement, not just topics. Pick a statement and the papers on each side light up. They are split by the kind of evidence behind them: their own data, a review, or argument.
    • It shows why the sides differ. It compares the study designs behind each side, for example how many papers on each side used posed faces and how many used spontaneous ones.
    • In the preview, nothing is generated while you use it. The reading was done ahead of time, so every answer is a list of papers you can check, not a paragraph of AI prose.
    • It’s honest about what it is: one researcher’s library, read with AI and spot-checked. It is not a meta-analysis (a formal statistical review of all the studies on a question).

    1. Ask the galaxy a question in plain words, such as “Do smiles show real enjoyment?” or “Are facial expressions of emotion recognised across cultures?”. You’ll see the statements it matches, with the papers that support, partly agree with or challenge each one. It works best with questions about what the research agrees or disagrees on.
    2. Turn the galaxy and point at a star to see which paper it is.
    3. Browse the library: look a paper up, narrow the list down, and show what you found on the map.
    4. Walk the map of the papers. Two papers sit close together when their full texts talk about the same things, so neighbourhoods form on their own. Click one to see the paper, what it argues and where it sits.

    Status

    • This is a live preview: a snapshot of the tool in development, taken in October 2026.
    • It maps more than 1,600 papers from my research library.
    • It is one researcher’s library, read with AI and spot-checked, not a meta-analysis.
    • The spot-check, in September 2026: 50 of the AI’s links between papers and statements were picked at random and re-read by a second AI pass, not by a person, and 44 held up. So treat any single link as a lead, and check the paper.
    • In the preview, a paper is named only if its DOI (its permanent publication link) has been checked against Crossref, the DOI registry. Other papers appear as unnamed points.

    How it’s builtDesigned and built by me with Claude Code since September 2026 · papers placed by how alike their full texts are and grouped into topics (TF-IDF, LSA, nearest-neighbour graph, Louvain communities, 3D t-SNE) · each AI-made link must point to a sentence in its paper, checked by a script · three.js and d3

    Face Value

    Build facial expressions from named movements on a 3D head, and see what the evidence says they mean.

    Because a face shouldn’t be taken at face value.

    Try the live preview Try Face Mirror

    Preview snapshot, October 2026 · opens the tool in development · about 3 MB, best on a laptop or tablet

    Updated November 2026

    Face Mirror names the movements of a face and hands it to Face Value, which shows what published studies link it to, kept apart by what people felt, what they posed and what observers saw. One added movement makes the textbook surprise face, found in only 6% of real surprise; then a smile built from named movements, and made-up tracking data played back on the head. Synthetic face and example data; screen recording, no sound; pointer added for clarity.

    Synthetic face: ICT Face Model Light from ICT-FaceKit (USC Institute for Creative Technologies), MIT licence.

    Paused because your device asks for less motion.

    About the numbers in the pictures. Each facial movement has a standard number in the Facial Action Coding System (FACS), which researchers use to describe faces. 12, for example, is the lip-corner pull of a smile. That’s what the “AU” numbers in the pictures are.

    Researchers and students who work with facial expressions, teams building or testing face-reading AI, and anyone who has wondered whether a smile always means happy.

    You switch facial movements on by name (a cheek raise, a nose wrinkle, lowered brows), and a realistic synthetic clay head makes the face. Beside it, a reading panel says:

    • what published studies link that face to;
    • how strong the evidence is;
    • which study each statement rests on.

    • It doesn’t tell you what someone feels. You build a face, and it shows what published studies found for it, how strong that evidence is, and where studies disagree.
    • It keeps three kinds of evidence apart: what people who felt an emotion actually did with their faces, what people did when asked to pose it, and what observers read into a face.
    • It shows how solid each reading is. “Evidence” says how strong the research behind it is. “Confidence” also asks whether anyone has studied this exact combination of movements. Combinations nobody has tested are labelled “untested”.
    • The public preview shows no real person. Every face is a synthetic 3D head.

    1. Build a face. Hover over a movement to preview it on the head; click to add it. Try the “textbook” surprise face (raised brows, wide eyes, dropped jaw) and see how often published studies found it in people who really were surprised.
    2. Step through face-tracking data, moment by moment. Load output from tools such as OpenFace, Py-Feat or AFFDEX, or try the built-in synthetic example. The head replays each moment, and lip movement that may just be speech is flagged.
    3. Follow any reading back to its source study. Every statement in the reading panel names the published study it rests on.
    4. Practise the statistics behind such studies in a built-in lab that exports matching R and Python code.

    Synthetic face: ICT Face Model Light from ICT-FaceKit (USC Institute for Creative Technologies), MIT licence.

    Status

    This is a live preview: a snapshot of the tool in development, taken in October 2026.

    • The evidence comes from other researchers’ published studies, each cited in the tool.
    • Face Value’s own readings and grades are still being developed and are not published results.
    • It accompanies a review of facial movements and emotion that is in progress.
    • It works best on a laptop or tablet.

    How it’s builtBuilt with Claude Code · WebGL clay head (three.js, ICT Face Model Light) driven by named facial movements · evidence base of published studies with its own scoring rules · reads OpenFace, Py-Feat, FaceReader, AFFDEX and MediaPipe output · statistics lab with R and Python export

    Self-Discovery

    Name what you feel, see your week, and test what really helps.

    Ask me for a demo See more screens

    I’ll show it to you over a video call.

    Updated November 2026

    Anyone who wants to understand their own moods and days, privately.

    • You check in a few times a day, in seconds: what you’re doing, how you feel, and what’s helping or getting in the way.
    • Over the weeks, Self-Discovery shows your week at a glance and finds what tends to go with your better and worse days.
    • It also helps you test a hunch fairly.

    • Words first. You start from a precise feeling (anxious, drained, content) rather than a number. Close feelings are shown side by side so you can tell them apart, and mood and energy are worked out from the words you choose.
    • Honest about uncertainty. Every pattern says how sure it is, shows the days behind it, and reminds you it’s a lead to test, not proof of cause.
    • From hunch to fair test. Turn a pattern into a small experiment, for example no coffee after 2 pm: the app picks the days at random and compares your sleep on those days with the rest.
    • Private by design.
      • There’s no account, no ads and no server. Your record stays on your own devices, and you can export everything whenever you like.
      • The Mac and iPhone versions are designed to sync directly over your home Wi-Fi, encrypted.
      • Optional weather lookups send only an approximate location to a public weather service.

    1. Check in in seconds: tap, or type a sentence and the app picks the matching options for you.
    2. Find the right word. Open a family of feelings and compare neighbours. For example, anxious usually lasts hours and is about something ahead; afraid usually lasts minutes and is about a clear threat.
    3. See your week: how you rated each day (for example, emotional wellbeing) against your usual range, with your feelings and sleep beneath it.
    4. Ask “why?”. See what tends to go with higher energy or a better mood, how sure the app is, and the individual days behind it.

    About the words

    • Some words, and the distinctions between close feelings, are inspired by Brené Brown’s Atlas of the Heart (2021).
    • Typical durations draw on research on how long emotions last (for example, Verduyn & Lavrijsen, 2015).
    • Research suggests that describing feelings precisely goes with handling them better, though most of that evidence shows a link, not cause and effect.
    • These words are a guide for describing experience, not a diagnosis. When heavy feelings come up, the app points to support.

    Status

    In development for Mac. An early iPhone version exists but isn’t ready yet. Not released. Every screen shown here uses made-up demo data.

    How it’s builtBuilt with Claude Code since October 2026 · native SwiftUI app for Mac (iPhone version in progress) · statistics computed on the device · encrypted device-to-device sync (tested over the local network) · automated tests
